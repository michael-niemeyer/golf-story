View 1Beginning · the question

Your textbook is wrong about golf

Intro physics says a ball launched at speed v0 and angle θ0 travels R = v02 sin 2θ0 / g, and that 45° is best. Here a ball leaves the club at the 2023 PGA Tour driver average: 171 mph with 2545 rpm of backspin.

Move the launch angle. At the tour's real launch angle (about 10°) the ball in air out-carries the vacuum ball, and the measured tour average beats the vacuum answer too. Push toward 45° and the order flips: the vacuum ball flies hundreds of yards farther.

Air both helps and hurts. Views 2 and 3 show the two forces responsible.

View 2Middle · mechanism

Three forces act on the ball

Weight mg pulls down and never changes. Drag points against the ball's motion through the air. Lift points at right angles to that motion; backspin tilts it upward.

Drag and lift both grow with the square of air speed: F = ½ ρ A C u2. Early in a drive the ball is so fast that lift is bigger than its weight, so it keeps climbing. As it slows, both aerodynamic forces shrink and gravity takes over.

Play the flight, step it, drag the time slider, or click the force chart. The arrows share one scale in newtons, and the path is drawn at true shape so the angles are real. The time cursor is shared with View 3.

View 3Middle · mechanism

Where lift and drag come from

How strongly the air pushes depends on two dimensionless numbers. The spin ratio S = rω/u compares the surface speed of the spinning ball to its speed through the air. The Reynolds number Re = ρuD/μ says how turbulent the flow is.

Smits and Smith (1994) measured a dimpled ball in a wind tunnel and fitted CL = 0.54 S0.4 and a drag coefficient that rises with S and wobbles with Re. During a drive, speed falls faster than spin, so S rises: watch the blue marker slide right along the curves.

Dimples trip the boundary layer into turbulence. The flow then clings to the back of the ball longer, the wake narrows, and drag drops. Switch to a smooth sphere (drag coefficient about 0.5) to see how much carry the dimples buy.

The grey band is the range the published fit was made for (S 0.08–0.20). Outside it, the curves are extrapolation.

View 5Middle · exploration

The optimal launch window

For a fixed ball speed, which mix of launch angle and spin carries farthest? Each map holds 3,721 simulated shots (61 angles × 61 spin rates), precomputed in Python at every ball speed that appears in the TrackMan tables.

Too little spin and the ball has no lift and falls out of the sky. Too much and drag grows and the ball balloons. The best carry sits in between, along a ridge where a lower launch needs more spin and a higher launch needs less.

Darker = longer carry. The color scale covers the top 60 yd of each map; anything shorter shares the lightest color. White lines are contours every 10 yd (or 10 m). Orange markers are measured clubs at this exact ball speed.

Wind, altitude and curve in three dimensions

Tilting the spin axis tilts the lift sideways, so the ball curves: a fade for a right-handed golfer when the axis tilts right. Wind changes the ball's speed relative to the air, and altitude thins the air. Save two scenarios as A and B to compare them. This is the one view where 3-D earns its place, because the curve really happens sideways.

What the evidence supports

    Limitations

    • The aerodynamic fit is from one 1990s two-piece ball in one wind tunnel, and Smits & Smith state it is only for driver shots (Re 70,000–210,000, S 0.08–0.20). Every iron in the table spends most of its flight outside that range.
    • Point-mass model: no ball deformation, no roll after landing, flat ground, uniform wind at all heights, constant air temperature.
    • TrackMan values are averages over many shots. Because flight is nonlinear, the carry of the average launch need not equal the average carry, so some disagreement is expected even from a perfect model.
    • Calibration used one target (PGA driver carry) and two constants, and the best fit sits on the edge of the range the paper allows. The other 22 clubs are held out.
